SN1 Mechanism

Unimolecular Nucleophilic Substitution • Stepwise Ionization • Planar Intermediate

1. Overview & Kinetics

The SN1 (Substitution, Nucleophilic, Unimolecular) reaction is a two-step substitution process that proceeds via a discrete carbocation intermediate. Because the initial heterolytic bond cleavage (ionization) is the slowest event, the reaction rate is strictly governed by substrate concentration alone.

Rate Law & Molecularity

Rate = k[Substrate] — First-order overall. Changing the concentration or nucleophilic power of the incoming nucleophile does not affect the reaction rate.

2. Step-by-Step Mechanism

Step 1: Heterolytic Bond Cleavage (Rate-Determining Step)

The leaving group departs with both bonding electrons. This heterolysis rehybridizes the central carbon from tetrahedral sp3 to trigonal planar sp2 with an unhybridized, empty p orbital.

Step 2: Nucleophilic Attack on Planar Carbocation (Fast Step)

The incoming nucleophile attacks the planar cation. Because both faces of the empty p orbital are exposed, attack occurs from above or below, leading to stereochemical racemization.

3. Stereochemistry: Racemization vs. Ion-Pair Shielding

Because the intermediate carbocation is sp2 hybridized and planar, nucleophiles attack both faces with theoretically identical probabilities, creating a 50:50 mixture of enantiomers (racemization) and loss of optical activity.

The Ion-Pair Reality (Partial Inversion Excess)

In solution, the departing leaving group remains briefly in an intimate ion pair ([R+ ••• LG−]), partially shielding the departure face. The nucleophile preferentially attacks the opposite side, typically resulting in 55% to 70% inversion and 30% to 45% retention.

4. Controlling Factors

1. Substrate Class

Requires carbocation stabilization via hyperconjugation & induction: 3° >> 2°. Methyl and primary substrates never undergo SN1.

2. Nucleophile Strength

Does not participate in the rate-determining step. Weak, neutral nucleophiles (H2O, ROH) are optimal and prevent competing E2.

3. Leaving Group Quality

Must be stable as an anion (weak base). Good leaving groups: I− > Br− > Cl− ≈ OTs−. OH− must be protonated to H2O.

4. Solvent Effects

Polar protic solvents (H2O, EtOH) dramatically accelerate SN1 by solvating and stabilizing both R+ and LG− via H-bonding.

5. Cross-Mechanism Relationships

Compare SN1 with other fundamental pathways:

  • SN2 Mechanism — One-step concerted backside attack; favored by methyl/1° substrates and strong nucleophiles in polar aprotic solvents.
  • E1 Mechanism — Direct competitor; shares the exact same carbocation intermediate. High temperature tips the balance to E1.
  • E2 Mechanism — Concerted bimolecular elimination requiring a strong base and anti-periplanar geometry.
