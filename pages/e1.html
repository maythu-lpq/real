E1 Mechanism

Unimolecular Elimination • Carbocation Intermediate • Zaitsev Regioselectivity

1. Overview & Kinetics

E1 (Elimination, Unimolecular) is a two-step elimination process. In Step 1, the leaving group ionizes away to generate a carbocation intermediate (identical to the first step of SN1). In Step 2, a weak base abstracts a β-proton, and the electron pair collapses to form a new carbon–carbon π bond (alkene).

Rate Law & First-Order Kinetics

Rate = k[Substrate] — Because the base is not involved in the rate-determining ionization, its concentration does not appear in the rate equation. Doubling [Substrate] doubles the rate; altering [Base] has zero effect.

2. Step-by-Step Mechanism

Step 1: Heterolytic Bond Cleavage (Slow, Rate-Determining)

The leaving group departs taking the electron pair, producing a planar sp2 carbocation at the α-carbon.

Step 2: Base Abstraction of β-Hydrogen (Fast)

A weak base (often solvent such as H2O or EtOH) abstracts a hydrogen from an adjacent β-carbon. The electrons from the broken Cβ–H bond flow directly into the empty p orbital on Cα, forging the C=C π bond.

3. Regiochemistry & Stereochemistry

Zaitsev's Rule (More Substituted Alkene Major)

When multiple β-carbons carry hydrogens, elimination follows Zaitsev's rule: the more substituted, thermodynamically more stable alkene predominates. Greater hyperconjugation and alkyl inductive donation lower the transition state energy for β-proton abstraction.

Lack of Stereospecificity & E/Z Mixtures

Unlike E2, E1 has no strict anti-periplanar requirement. Because the carbocation intermediate is free to rotate around its single bond before deprotonation, both E (trans) and Z (cis) alkenes can form. The less sterically crowded E-isomer typically predominates.

Carbocation Rearrangements

Because a real carbocation intermediate exists, 1,2-hydride shifts and 1,2-alkyl shifts will occur whenever a secondary carbocation can rearrange into a more stable tertiary carbocation.

4. Controlling Factors & SN1 Competition

1. Substrate Class

Requires carbocation stability: 3° >> 2°. Primary and methyl substrates never undergo E1.

2. Base & Nucleophile

Weak bases (solvent like H2O, EtOH) suffice. Strong bases divert the pathway to E2.

3. Temperature (The Deciding Factor)

High temperature (Δ, reflux) strongly favors E1 over SN1 due to favorable entropy (ΔS > 0 for elimination: 2 molecules → 3 products).

4. Solvent Effects

Polar protic solvents (water, alcohols) stabilize the carbocation intermediate and departing anion via solvation shells.

5. Cross-Mechanism Relationships

Compare E1 with other pathways:

  • SN1 Mechanism — Shares the identical carbocation intermediate. Room temperature favors SN1; heat favors E1.
  • E2 Mechanism — Concerted bimolecular elimination requiring strong base and anti-periplanar geometry.
  • SN2 Mechanism — Concerted substitution favored on unhindered substrates.
