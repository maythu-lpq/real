# E1 Elimination — Unimolecular Elimination

> **Source file:** `wiki/e1.md` | Part of the [Organic Chemistry Reaction Mechanism Wiki](index.md)

---

## Overview

**E1 (Elimination, Unimolecular)** is a two-step reaction mechanism in which a substrate loses a leaving group and a β-hydrogen to form a **carbon–carbon π bond (alkene)**. The "1" in E1 refers to the **unimolecular** nature of the rate-determining step — only the substrate concentration appears in the rate law.

E1 proceeds through a **carbocation intermediate**, making it mechanistically identical to SN1 up through the first step. Because of this shared intermediate, **E1 and SN1 always compete** under the same conditions. The outcome (substitution vs. elimination) depends primarily on **temperature** and the nature of the solvent/base present.

### Key Characteristics at a Glance

| Feature | E1 |
|---|---|
| Steps | 2 (ionization, then deprotonation) |
| Intermediate | Carbocation |
| Rate order | First-order (unimolecular) |
| Substrate preference | 3° >> 2°; never 1° or methyl |
| Base required | Weak (often just the solvent) |
| Solvent | Polar protic (H₂O, ROH) |
| Stereochemistry | Non-stereospecific; both E and Z possible |
| Competing reaction | SN1 (always) |
| Regiochemistry | Zaitsev's Rule — more substituted alkene predominates |

> **Key concept:** E1 and SN1 share the **same carbocation intermediate**. Every SN1 reaction is accompanied by some E1, and vice versa. The question is always which pathway the carbocation takes: nucleophilic attack (→ substitution) or proton loss (→ elimination).

---

## Kinetics & Rate Law

E1 is a **first-order** reaction overall:

```
Rate = k[substrate]
```

### Rate-Determining Step

The **rate-determining step** is **ionization** — the heterolytic cleavage of the C–LG bond to generate the carbocation intermediate. This step is identical to Step 1 of SN1.

- Because the base/nucleophile is **not** involved in the slow step, its concentration does **not** appear in the rate law.
- Doubling the substrate concentration → rate doubles.
- Changing base concentration → **no effect** on rate.

### Energy Profile

The E1 reaction has a **two-humped energy diagram** (two transition states, one intermediate):

```
Energy
  |         TS1              TS2
  |          *               *
  |         / \             / \
  |        /   \           /   \
  |       /     \         /     \
  |      /       * (C⁺) *       \
  |     /      Carbocation        \
  |----/                           \----
  |  Reactant                      Alkene product
  |
  +----------------------------------------> Reaction coordinate
```

- **TS1** = C–LG bond breaking (high energy; rate-determining)
- **Carbocation** = intermediate (energy trough between TS1 and TS2)
- **TS2** = C–H bond breaking / π bond formation (lower energy; fast step)

> **Tip:** Because TS2 is fast relative to TS1, the regioselectivity of the alkene product is determined by the relative stability of possible transition states in Step 2 — leading to **Zaitsev's Rule**.

## Step-by-Step Mechanism

### Step 1: Ionization (Rate-Determining)

The leaving group departs **heterolytically**, taking both electrons from the C–LG bond. This generates a **carbocation** at the carbon that bore the leaving group (the α-carbon).

```
         LG                              ⊕
    —C—C—          slow, rate-det.   —C—C—     +   :LG⁻
    |   |          ————————————>     |   |
    H              (polar protic     H
  (β-C) (α-C)       solvent)      (β-C) (α-C)
```

**Driving forces for ionization:**
- Good leaving group (stable as anion after departure)
- Stable carbocation (3° > 2° > 1°; resonance or hyperconjugation)
- Polar protic solvent (H-bond solvation of LG⁻ and stabilization of C⁺)

> **Important:** The carbocation intermediate can undergo **SN1** (nucleophilic attack) **or E1** (proton loss). Both pathways are simultaneously available.

### Step 2: Deprotonation (Fast)

A base — often just the **solvent molecule** (H₂O or ROH) — abstracts a **β-hydrogen** (a proton on a carbon adjacent to the carbocation). The electrons from the C–H bond flow toward the carbocation, forming the **π bond** of the alkene.

```
         B:          H                       B—H
          \           |                        +
    —C—C⊕  →    —C = C—          (alkene product)
    |             
  (β-C) (α-C)
```

**Curved arrow summary:**
1. Base lone pair attacks the β-H → breaks C–H bond
2. Electrons from C–H bond → form new C=C π bond
3. Carbocation at α-C is quenched

### Regiochemistry: Zaitsev's Rule

When more than one β-carbon is available (i.e., the carbocation has β-hydrogens on different carbons), **Zaitsev's Rule** predicts the major product:

> **Zaitsev's Rule:** In E1 (and E2) eliminations, the **more substituted alkene** (more alkyl substituents on the double bond) is the thermodynamically preferred and kinetically major product.

**Rationale:** The more substituted alkene is more stable due to **hyperconjugation** (more C–H bonds aligned with the π system) and **inductive donation** from alkyl groups. TS2 leading to the more substituted alkene is **lower in energy**.

**Example — 2-methylbutan-2-ol under acidic conditions (E1):**

```
         H₃PO₄, heat
(CH₃)₃C–CH₂CH₃  ————————>  (CH₃)₂C=CHCH₃   (major, Zaitsev)
                                 +
                             CH₂=C(CH₃)CH₂CH₃  (minor)
```

The **trisubstituted** alkene (2-methyl-2-butene) is the major product; the **disubstituted** terminal alkene (2-methyl-1-butene) is minor.

## Stereochemistry

### No Strict Geometric Requirement

Unlike **E2**, which requires a **strict anti-periplanar** arrangement of the β-H and leaving group, E1 imposes **no geometric constraint** in the rate-determining step. Ionization produces a planar sp² carbocation; the subsequent deprotonation can occur from any accessible β-H regardless of dihedral angle.

### E/Z Alkene Mixtures

Because the intermediate carbocation allows free rotation around the remaining C–C bond before deprotonation, E1 can produce **both E (trans) and Z (cis) alkene isomers**:

- The **E (trans)** alkene is typically more stable and usually predominates (thermodynamic preference).
- The **Z (cis)** alkene is the minor product.

> **Contrast with E2:** E2 is **stereospecific** — the anti-periplanar geometry locks which diastereomer forms. E1 is **not stereospecific** and gives mixtures.

### Carbocation Rearrangements

Because E1 proceeds through a carbocation intermediate, **hydride shifts** and **methyl shifts** (1,2-shifts) can occur before deprotonation if they lead to a more stable (higher-order) carbocation:

```
     ⊕                       ⊕
—CH₂—C—  ——[1,2-H shift]——>  —CH—C—    (more stable if 3° forms)
```

This can produce **rearranged alkene products** that cannot be explained by simple E2 or SN2 pathways — a diagnostic hallmark of E1 (and SN1).

> **Diagnostic clue:** Rearranged products (skeletal rearrangements) are strong evidence for a carbocation intermediate and therefore for an E1 (or SN1) mechanism.

## Controlling Factors

### Substrate

**Substrate structure is the single most important factor determining whether E1 can occur.**

| Substrate Class | E1 Feasibility |
|---|---|
| Tertiary (3°) | ✅ Strongly favored — most stable carbocation |
| Secondary (2°) | ⚠️ Moderate — only with good LG and polar protic solvent |
| Primary (1°) | ❌ Not feasible — primary carbocations are too unstable |
| Methyl | ❌ Completely unfeasible |

**Structural features that promote E1:**
- **High substitution** at the α-carbon (stabilizes carbocation via hyperconjugation and inductive donation)
- **Resonance stabilization** of the carbocation (allylic, benzylic systems)
- **Branching** that offers multiple β-hydrogens for Zaitsev elimination

> **Rule of thumb:** If the substrate cannot form a reasonably stable carbocation (2° aliphatic minimum; ideally 3°), E1 is not a viable pathway.

**Examples by substrate class:**
```
(CH₃)₃CBr        →  E1 readily (tertiary; stable 3° carbocation)
(CH₃)₂CHBr       →  E1 possible (secondary; slower ionization)
CH₃CH₂Br         →  E1 not observed (primary carbocation too unstable)
```

### Nucleophile / Base

In E1, the base abstracts the β-hydrogen in the **fast** Step 2. Because this step is not rate-determining:

- **Weak bases** are sufficient — even solvent molecules (H₂O, EtOH, MeOH) can serve as the base.
- **Base concentration** does not affect the rate.
- **Strong bases** are **not required** (and in fact, strong base + substrate → E2 instead).

**Temperature is a critical factor:**
- **Higher temperature** shifts the product ratio toward **elimination** (E1) rather than substitution (SN1).
- Elimination has a larger activation energy than substitution on the carbocation intermediate; therefore entropy and temperature favor elimination at higher temperatures.

> **Practical guide:**
> - Weak base + polar protic solvent + 3° substrate + **room temperature** → SN1 major
> - Weak base + polar protic solvent + 3° substrate + **heat** → E1 major

| Condition | Favored Pathway |
|---|---|
| H₂O, 25 °C, 3° substrate | SN1 |
| H₂O, 100 °C (reflux), 3° substrate | E1 |
| Strong base (NaOH, KOtBu), any temperature | E2 |

### Leaving Group

E1 requires the **same leaving group quality as SN1** — the leaving group must be able to depart as a stable species (anion or neutral molecule) during ionization:

**Good leaving groups for E1:**
| Leaving Group | Species After Departure | Relative Reactivity |
|---|---|---|
| I⁻ (iodide) | Weak base, stable anion | Best |
| Br⁻ (bromide) | Weak base, stable anion | Excellent |
| Cl⁻ (chloride) | Moderate base, stable anion | Good |
| OTs⁻ (tosylate) | Very stable anion | Excellent |
| OTf⁻ (triflate) | Extremely stable anion | Best |
| H₂O (from protonated OH) | Neutral, stable | Excellent |

**Poor leaving groups (E1 not possible):**
- –OH (not protonated), –OR, –NH₂, –F (fluoride is too strongly bound)

> **Key principle:** Hydroxyl groups can be converted to good leaving groups by **protonation** under acidic conditions (H₂SO₄, H₃PO₄, TsOH), enabling E1 of alcohols. This is the basis of **acid-catalyzed dehydration of alcohols** to alkenes.

**Acid-catalyzed alcohol dehydration (E1 mechanism):**
```
Step 1: ROH  +  H⁺  →  ROH₂⁺      (protonation makes OH₂ a good LG)
Step 2: ROH₂⁺  →  R⁺  +  H₂O      (ionization; rate-determining)
Step 3: R⁺  +  base  →  alkene  +  BH⁺  (deprotonation; fast)
```

### Solvent Effects

E1 requires **polar protic solvents** — the same conditions that favor SN1:

| Solvent type | Effect on E1 |
|---|---|
| Polar protic (H₂O, ROH, AcOH) | ✅ Strongly favored — stabilizes ions via H-bonding and solvation |
| Polar aprotic (DMSO, DMF, acetone) | ❌ Disfavored — SN2 favored instead |
| Nonpolar (hexane, Et₂O) | ❌ Completely disfavored — cannot stabilize ionic intermediates |

**Why polar protic solvents favor E1:**
1. **Solvation of LG⁻** — polar protic solvents H-bond to the departing anion, stabilizing the transition state for ionization.
2. **Stabilization of carbocation** — the high dielectric constant of polar protic solvents disperses the positive charge.
3. **Solvent as base** — H₂O and ROH can act as the base in Step 2 (they are weak bases, perfectly suited for E1).

## E1 vs. SN1 Competition

Because E1 and SN1 share the **same carbocation intermediate**, they always compete. The carbocation can either:
- **Be attacked by a nucleophile** → SN1 product (substitution)
- **Lose a β-proton to a base** → E1 product (elimination)

### Factors That Shift the Balance

| Factor | Favors SN1 | Favors E1 |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Lower temperature | Higher temperature (heat/reflux) |
| Nucleophile present | Strong nucleophile | Weak/no nucleophile (solvent only) |
| Substrate branching | Less branched | More branched (more β-H available) |
| Base strength | No base | Weak base at high T |

> **Summary rule:** To maximize E1, use a **tertiary substrate**, a **polar protic solvent**, **weak base** (e.g., H₂O or ROH), and **heat**. To maximize SN1, use the same conditions but at **lower temperature**.

### Example: tert-Butyl Bromide in Water/Ethanol

```
(CH₃)₃CBr   +   H₂O/EtOH
      |
      |  [SN1 / E1 competition through (CH₃)₃C⁺ intermediate]
      |
      ├──> (CH₃)₃COH  or  (CH₃)₃COEt   [SN1 products]
      └──> (CH₃)₂C=CH₂  (isobutylene)   [E1 product]
```

- At 25 °C: predominantly SN1 (substitution)
- At reflux (>80 °C): E1 product (isobutylene) becomes major

## Related Mechanisms

Understanding E1 is inseparable from understanding its three sibling reactions:

| Mechanism | Type | Key Feature | Link |
|---|---|---|---|
| SN1 | Substitution, unimolecular | Same carbocation intermediate; substitution product | [SN1 Mechanism](sn1.md) |
| SN2 | Substitution, bimolecular | Concerted backside attack; inversion; no carbocation | [SN2 Mechanism](sn2.md) |
| E2 | Elimination, bimolecular | Concerted; anti-periplanar; strong base required; stereospecific | [E2 Mechanism](e2.md) |

### Choosing Between Mechanisms — Decision Guide

```
Is the substrate 3° or 2°?
 ├── YES → Is a strong base present (NaOH, KOtBu, LDA)?
 │          ├── YES → E2 (elimination, stereospecific)
 │          └── NO  → Is temperature high (reflux)?
 │                     ├── YES → E1 major (elimination)
 │                     └── NO  → SN1 major (substitution)
 └── NO (1° or methyl) → Is nucleophile strong?
            ├── YES → SN2
            └── NO  → No good reaction
```

### Key Comparisons

**E1 vs. E2:**
- E1: weak base, polar protic, 3°, non-stereospecific, proceeds via carbocation
- E2: strong base, can be polar aprotic, all degrees (1°–3°), anti-periplanar, stereospecific, concerted (no carbocation)

**E1 vs. SN1:**
- Both: 3° substrate, weak nucleophile/base, polar protic, carbocation intermediate
- Distinguish by: temperature (heat → E1) and alkene vs. substituted product analysis

> **Mnemonic:** "**E1 = SN1 minus the nucleophile**" — same conditions, same intermediate, but instead of substitution, the carbocation loses a proton to give an alkene.
