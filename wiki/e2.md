# E2 Elimination — Bimolecular Elimination

## Overview

E2 (Elimination, Bimolecular) is a **concerted, single-step** elimination reaction in which a strong base abstracts a β-hydrogen **simultaneously** as the leaving group departs from the α-carbon, forming a carbon–carbon π bond (alkene) in one elementary step.

**General reaction:**

```
        Base
         |
  β-H — C — C — LG   →   C=C  +  H-Base  +  LG⁻
         α
```

Key features at a glance:

| Feature | E2 |
|---|---|
| Steps | 1 (concerted) |
| Intermediate | None |
| Rate | Second-order: Rate = k[substrate][base] |
| Base required | Strong, often bulky |
| Stereochemistry | Anti-periplanar (strict geometric requirement) |
| Regioselectivity | Zaitsev (or Hofmann with bulky base) |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> E2 has **no carbocation intermediate** — all three bond changes (C–H breaking, C=C forming, C–LG breaking) occur simultaneously at a single transition state. This is the fundamental distinction from [E1 Elimination](e1.md).

---

## Kinetics & Rate Law

E2 is a **bimolecular** process. Both the substrate and the base are involved in the rate-determining (and only) transition state.

**Rate Law:**

```
Rate = k[substrate][base]
```

- **Second-order overall**: first-order in substrate, first-order in base.
- Doubling [base] doubles the rate; doubling [substrate] doubles the rate.
- Increasing the concentration of base **always** increases the E2 rate — a key diagnostic difference from E1.

**Energy profile:**

E2 has a **single transition state** and **no intermediate**. The reaction coordinate shows one energy maximum corresponding to the concerted, six-electron, cyclic-like transition state:

```
Energy
  |           ‡
  |          /|\
  |         / | \
  |        /  |  \
  |       /   |   \
  |------/    |    \------
  |   Reactants        Products
  |
  +---------------------------------> Reaction Coordinate
```

The activation energy reflects the simultaneous cost of:
1. Partial breaking of the C–H bond (β-carbon).
2. Partial forming of the C=C π bond.
3. Partial breaking of the C–LG bond (α-carbon).

> [!TIP]
> Because rate depends on [base], E2 can be promoted over E1/SN1 simply by using a **high concentration of a strong base**.

## Step-by-Step Mechanism

E2 is a **single concerted step** — there is no stepwise mechanism to describe, only a single transition state.

### The Concerted Transition State

```
       δ-                   δ-
  Base···H — Cβ — Cα — LG
              ↑
        (all bonds change simultaneously)
```

**What happens at the transition state:**

1. **C–H bond breaks**: The strong base begins abstracting the β-hydrogen. The C–H bond elongates and becomes partially broken.
2. **C=C π bond forms**: The electrons from the C–H bond begin to flow into the region between Cβ and Cα, forming the incipient π bond.
3. **C–LG bond breaks**: Simultaneously, the leaving group begins to depart from Cα, taking both electrons from the C–LG bond.

All three events are coupled and occur in concert — none precedes the other.

### Visual Summary

```
Step 1 (and only step):

   B:  +  H–Cβ–Cα–LG   →   [B···H···Cβ···Cα···LG]‡  →   B–H  +  Cβ=Cα  +  LG⁻
           |   |                  Transition State
```

**Comparison to E1:**

| Feature | E2 | E1 |
|---|---|---|
| Steps | 1 | 2 |
| Intermediate | None | Carbocation |
| Base requirement | Strong | Weak (even solvent) |
| Rate | k[S][B] | k[S] |
| Stereochemistry | Strict anti-periplanar | No strict requirement |

> [!NOTE]
> Because E2 is concerted, **carbocation rearrangements do not occur** — there is no carbocation intermediate that could rearrange via hydride or alkyl shifts.

## Stereochemistry

E2 has the most demanding and consequential stereochemical requirement of all four core mechanisms.

### The Anti-Periplanar Requirement

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The β-H and the leaving group **must be anti-periplanar** — they must lie in the same plane with a **180° dihedral angle** between them (trans to each other). This is a strict geometric requirement for E2.

**Why anti-periplanar?**
- The concerted mechanism requires orbital overlap: the σ*(C–LG) antibonding orbital must overlap with the C–H σ bond being broken.
- Maximum orbital overlap occurs when the C–H and C–LG bonds are anti (180°), coplanar and pointing in opposite directions.
- Syn-periplanar (0°) elimination is geometrically possible but far less common because eclipsed conformations are higher in energy.

```
Anti-periplanar (required geometry):

      H        (top)
      |
  Cβ–Cα
      |
      LG       (bottom, anti to H)

Dihedral angle H–Cβ–Cα–LG = 180°
```

### Consequence: Stereospecificity of Product Alkene

The anti-periplanar requirement makes E2 **stereospecific** — the geometry of the starting material determines the geometry (E or Z) of the alkene product.

**Example — Meso vs (2R,3R) starting material:**

- **Meso-2,3-dibromobutane + KOH** → anti-periplanar conformer has the two Br anti, one H anti to one Br → product: **trans (E)-2-butene** (Br's constrain geometry)
- **(2R,3R)-2,3-dibromobutane + KOH** → anti conformer produces **cis (Z)-2-butene**

> [!TIP]
> To predict the E2 product's E/Z geometry: draw the Newman projection of the bond undergoing elimination, rotate to place H and LG anti, then read off which groups end up cis/trans in the alkene.

### Cyclohexane Systems: Diaxial Requirement

In cyclohexane rings, E2 imposes an especially important conformational constraint:

> [!IMPORTANT]
> In a cyclohexane, E2 requires the β-H and the leaving group to both be in **axial positions** (diaxial conformation). Equatorial–equatorial or axial–equatorial arrangements cannot achieve anti-periplanar geometry.

**Implications:**
- A ring must adopt the chair conformation with both H and LG axial before E2 can occur.
- If the most stable chair has the LG equatorial, the ring must ring-flip to put LG axial — this costs energy and may slow the reaction.
- In some rigid systems (e.g., trans-4-tert-butylcyclohexyl tosylate), if no conformer can place H and LG diaxial simultaneously, E2 is severely retarded or impossible.

```
Cyclohexane chair — required geometry:

     H (axial)
     |
 ----C----
     |
     LG (axial, on opposite face)
```

### Zaitsev vs Hofmann Regioselectivity

When more than one type of β-hydrogen is available, E2 is **regioselective**:

| Rule | Base | Product | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zaitsev | Small base (EtO⁻, OH⁻) | More substituted alkene (more stable) | Base accesses any β-H; thermodynamic stability of alkene controls |
| Hofmann | Bulky base (tBuO⁻, DBU) | Less substituted alkene | Steric bulk blocks approach to internal β-H; external (less hindered) β-H is abstracted |

**Example:**
```
2-Bromobutane + KOEt   →  2-butene (Zaitsev, major) + 1-butene (minor)
2-Bromobutane + KOtBu  →  1-butene (Hofmann, major) + 2-butene (minor)
```

> [!NOTE]
> Zaitsev's rule holds when the base is small and unhindered. The Hofmann rule is a **steric effect of the base**, not of the substrate — the bulky base cannot easily approach the more-hindered internal β-hydrogens.

## Controlling Factors

### Substrate

- **Reactivity order:** 3° > 2° > 1°
- E2 works across all substrate classes if the base is strong enough — unlike E1, it does not require a stable carbocation.
- **Steric effects on competition with SN2:** Bulky (3°) substrates strongly favor E2 over SN2 because backside attack (SN2) is sterically blocked, while deprotonation (E2) is less sensitive to α-carbon bulk.
- **1° substrates:** E2 is possible with a very strong base (e.g., NaOtBu), though SN2 may still compete. Methyl substrates essentially only undergo SN2.

| Substrate Class | E2 Tendency | Competition |
|---|---|---|
| Methyl | Very low | SN2 dominates |
| 1° | Low–moderate | SN2 competes |
| 2° | Moderate | SN2/E2 mixture; depends on base and T |
| 3° | High | E2 strongly favored; SN2 sterically blocked |

> [!TIP]
> **High temperature** always shifts the competition toward elimination (E2) over substitution (SN2 or SN1) because elimination has a higher activation energy but a more positive ΔS (more product molecules formed).

### Nucleophile/Base

E2 requires a **strong base**. The base abstracts the β-H in the rate-determining (and only) transition state.

**Common E2 bases:**

| Base | Formula | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Sodium hydroxide | NaOH | Common, unhindered; Zaitsev product |
| Potassium hydroxide | KOH | Common; EtOH or H₂O solvent |
| Sodium ethoxide | NaOEt | Stronger than NaOH; EtOH solvent; Zaitsev |
| Potassium tert-butoxide | KOtBu | Bulky; strongly favors Hofmann (less substituted) product |
| DBU | 1,8-Diazabicyclo[5.4.0]undec-7-ene | Very bulky, non-nucleophilic; exclusively E2 |
| LDA | Lithium diisopropylamide | Extremely strong, bulky; often used for 1° or in synthesis |

**Bulky bases and the Hofmann product:**

When a bulky base such as KOtBu cannot easily approach sterically hindered internal β-H atoms, it preferentially abstracts the terminal (less substituted) β-H, giving the **less substituted alkene** (Hofmann product):

```
      CH₃                    H   H
       |                     |   |
  CH₃-C-CH₂-CH₃  + KOtBu →  C=C    (1-butene, Hofmann)
       |                     |   |
       Br                   CH₃  CH₂CH₃
```

> [!NOTE]
> The choice of base is the **single most powerful experimental handle** for controlling E2 regioselectivity. Use NaOEt for Zaitsev, KOtBu for Hofmann.

### Leaving Group

- E2 requires a **good leaving group** (same requirement as SN2).
- **Leaving group ability (best to worst):** I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ >> F⁻; also OTs⁻ (tosylate), OTf⁻ (triflate), OMs⁻ (mesylate).
- Poor leaving groups (OH, OR, NH₂) do not work in E2 without activation (e.g., protonation of OH to form OH₂⁺, or conversion to tosylate).
- The leaving group and β-H must be able to adopt the **anti-periplanar geometry** in the transition state — this is a conformational constraint that can override leaving group ability (see Stereochemistry section).

> [!TIP]
> To improve a poor leaving group for E2: convert –OH to –OTs (tosylate) using TsCl/pyridine. The tosylate is an excellent leaving group and E2 proceeds readily.

### Solvent Effects

| Solvent Type | Effect on E2 | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Polar protic | Generally acceptable; solvates LG⁻; may slightly solvate base | EtOH, t-BuOH, H₂O |
| Polar aprotic | Good for E2; does not solvate base (keeps it reactive) | DMSO, DMF, acetonitrile |
| High temperature | Strongly favors E2 over SN2 | — |
| Concentrated base | Strongly favors E2 over SN1/E1 | — |

**E2 vs SN2 — solvent and conditions summary:**

| Condition | Favors E2 | Favors SN2 |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | High | Low |
| Base/nucleophile | Strong base, bulky | Strong nucleophile, unhindered |
| Substrate | 2°, 3° | Methyl, 1° |
| Solvent | Either protic or aprotic | Polar aprotic |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The key to selecting E2 over SN2 experimentally: **use a strong, bulky base** (KOtBu) **at elevated temperature**. The bulk blocks backside attack (SN2 path), and heat shifts equilibrium toward the higher-entropy elimination products.

## Regioselectivity: Zaitsev vs Hofmann

When a substrate has β-hydrogens on multiple different carbons, E2 produces a mixture of alkene regioisomers. The major product depends on the base used.

### Zaitsev's Rule (Small Base)

> When a small, unhindered base is used (e.g., NaOEt, KOH), E2 produces the **more substituted alkene** as the major product.

**Rationale:** The more substituted alkene is more stable (greater hyperconjugation and alkyl inductive stabilization of the π bond). With a small base, all β-H atoms are accessible, and the more-substituted alkene's transition state is lower in energy (product-like TS, Hammond postulate for endothermic-like TS with strong bases).

```
2-Bromobutane + NaOEt/EtOH:
Major product: 2-butene (CH₃CH=CHCH₃) — Zaitsev
Minor product: 1-butene (CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃) — Hofmann
```

### Hofmann Elimination (Bulky Base)

> When a bulky base is used (e.g., KOtBu, DBU), E2 produces the **less substituted alkene** as the major product.

**Rationale:** The bulky base cannot easily access the internal β-H atoms (which are more sterically hindered), so it preferentially abstracts the less hindered terminal β-H, giving the less substituted (terminal) alkene.

```
2-Bromobutane + KOtBu/t-BuOH:
Major product: 1-butene (CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃) — Hofmann
Minor product: 2-butene (CH₃CH=CHCH₃) — Zaitsev
```

**Summary table:**

| Base | Steric Demand | Major Product | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| NaOH, KOH, NaOEt | Low | More substituted alkene | Zaitsev |
| KOtBu, DBU, LDA | High | Less substituted alkene | Hofmann |

> [!TIP]
> A useful mnemonic: **"Bulky base → less bulky product"** — the base is too bulky to reach internal H's, so it removes the terminal H, giving the terminal (less substituted) alkene.

## Related Mechanisms

E2 is one of four core substitution/elimination pathways. Understanding how they interrelate is essential for predicting products.

| Mechanism | Type | Key Feature | Link |
|---|---|---|---|
| SN1 | Substitution | Unimolecular; carbocation intermediate; racemization | [SN1 Mechanism](sn1.md) |
| SN2 | Substitution | Bimolecular; concerted; Walden inversion | [SN2 Mechanism](sn2.md) |
| E1 | Elimination | Unimolecular; carbocation intermediate; competes with SN1 | [E1 Elimination](e1.md) |
| **E2** | **Elimination** | **Bimolecular; concerted; anti-periplanar; this page** | — |

### Competition and Decision Framework

```
Given substrate + conditions → which mechanism?

1. Is the base/nucleophile strong?
   YES → SN2 or E2 (compare steric factors and temperature)
   NO  → SN1 or E1 (requires 3° or stabilized 2° substrate)

2. Is the substrate 3° (or bulky 2°)?
   YES + strong base + high T → E2 (steric block on SN2)
   YES + weak nucleophile    → SN1/E1

3. Is the base bulky (KOtBu)?
   YES → E2, Hofmann product

4. Is temperature high?
   YES → Favor elimination (E2 or E1)

5. Is solvent polar aprotic?
   YES → Favors SN2 (or E2 if conditions match)
   NO (polar protic) → Favors SN1/E1 or E2 depending on base strength
```

> [!NOTE]
> E2 and [SN2](sn2.md) always compete when a strong base/nucleophile is present. The balance shifts toward E2 with: higher temperature, bulkier substrate, bulkier base, or lower nucleophilicity of the base relative to its base strength.

> [!NOTE]
> E2 differs from [E1](e1.md) in that it requires a strong base and shows second-order kinetics, while E1 only requires ionization (first-order) and can proceed with a weak base or even solvent.
